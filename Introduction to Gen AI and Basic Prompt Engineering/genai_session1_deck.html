Part 1 · The Story of AI

Where This All Began

Every technology has an origin story. This one doesn't start with a computer — it starts with a question.

Before we open up a large language model, it helps to see the seventy-year path that led to it — because every idea on that path is still alive inside the tool you'll use today.

1950

Can a Machine Think?

Alan Turing sidesteps the philosophical question and proposes a practical test instead: the Imitation Game. A human judge holds a typed conversation with two unseen partners — one human, one machine. If the judge can't reliably tell which is which, the machine passes.

Example exchange

Judge: "Are you a machine?"

Respondent: "That's an odd thing to ask someone you've just met."

No test of "thinking" — just a test of whether the conversation feels indistinguishable from a person's. It's the first time a machine's intelligence is measured by how it talks, not what it computes — the exact question every chatbot today is still being judged against.

1955–56 · Dartmouth Conference

The Term "Artificial Intelligence" Is Coined

John McCarthy (Stanford), later called the Father of AI, coins the term in a 1955 research proposal for a summer workshop held at Dartmouth College in 1956. His definition still holds up: "The science and engineering of making intelligent machines." The goal, in his words, was to build systems that could simulate every aspect of learning or any other feature of intelligence.

The full journey

From a Named Idea to a Working Machine

Naming a field is one thing. Getting a machine to actually improve on its own — that took a board game.
1950s–60s

Statistics and hand-written rules

Early "intelligence" meant counting patterns in data and encoding human expertise as rigid if-this-then-that recipes — ELIZA (1966) simulated a therapist from scripts; expert systems like MYCIN (1970s) diagnosed infections from a rulebook written by doctors.

1959

"Machine Learning" is coined — Arthur Samuel

Samuel's checkers-playing program improved by playing itself thousands of times, rather than being handed more rules. Learning from data, not from a recipe — that's the shift ML represents.

1980s–2000s

ML goes to work

Spam filters, credit-risk scoring, product recommendations — ML starts making everyday business decisions, learned from historical data rather than programmed by hand.

1943 → 2012

Deep Learning's long run-up

The idea of an artificial "neuron" dates to 1943; the training technique (backpropagation) arrives in 1986; progress then stalls for years. In 2012, a deep neural network called AlexNet wins the ImageNet image-recognition competition by a landslide — proof that with enough data and computing power, layered networks outperform everything before them.

1960s → today

Two specialists emerge: Computer Vision & NLP

Computer Vision teaches machines to see — from 1960s shape-recognition experiments to today's assembly-line defect cameras. NLP teaches machines language — from a 1954 machine-translation demo to today's sentiment analysis of customer reviews.

2017

The Transformer — "Attention Is All You Need"

A new architecture lets a model weigh every word against every other word in a passage at once, instead of reading strictly one word after another. This is the moment language models learn to hold an entire page in mind.

2018–2022

The Large Language Model is born

GPT-1, GPT-2, GPT-3 scale the Transformer up on ever larger volumes of text. In November 2022, ChatGPT puts that capability into a plain chat box, and a hundred million people try it within months.

2023–2026

Where we are now

Models that see, hear, reason step-by-step before answering, and take actions on your behalf — which is exactly where the rest of today's session, and the next three, begin.

Part 2 · Inside the LLM

What Is a Language Model?

History explains where it came from. Now let's open one up.

A language model is a system trained to predict the next most likely word (token), given everything that came before it. That is the entire trick — repeated, extremely well, at enormous scale.

Example

Your phone's keyboard already does this: type "I am currently..." and it suggests "at the gym." A language model does the same thing — it has simply read far more text before making that guess.

The "L" in LLM

What Does "Large" Actually Mean?

Scale, across three dimensions at once:

Parameters

The adjustable internal "dials" the model tunes during training — modern models have tens to hundreds of billions of them.

Training data

Trillions of words, drawn from books, code, articles, and the public web — far more than any one person could read in several lifetimes.

Compute

Specialised chips (GPUs) running for weeks or months to find the right value for every one of those parameters.

Example

If a smaller, task-specific model is like an employee trained only on your company's manual, a "large" model is like a new hire who has already read every manual, forum post, and news article on the internet — broad, but still needing your specific instructions to be useful to you.

A closer look

Tokenization

Tokenization is the process of breaking text into the small chunks — tokens — that a model actually reads and generates. A token might be a whole word, a piece of a word, or even just a punctuation mark; words are rarely kept whole.

We need it because a neural network can't read letters or words directly — it only works with numbers. Tokenization is the translation step: it chops text into a fixed vocabulary of pieces, and every piece maps to a number the model can compute with. It's also directly why you're billed per token, not per word.

Example
unbelievable

"Unbelievable" becomes three pieces, not one. Uncommon words split into odd fragments — which is exactly why a model can sometimes stumble counting the letters in a word like "strawberry": it isn't reading letter by letter, it's reading in these puzzle-piece chunks.

Not all LLMs are the same

Types of LLMs

Causal / autoregressive

Predicts strictly left-to-right, never peeking at words that come later. Where it applies: nearly every chat model in use today — GPT, Claude, Gemini, Qwen, and Llama are all built this way underneath.

Dense model

Every parameter activates for every request. Example: Meta's Llama 3, Mistral 7B, and the original GPT-3 all run their full network on every single prompt, no shortcuts taken.

Mixture-of-Experts

Only relevant "expert" sub-networks switch on per request. Example: Mistral's Mixtral, DeepSeek-V3, and Qwen's MoE models route this way explicitly; GPT-4 and Gemini are widely reported to use a similar approach.

"Thinking" / reasoning model

Works through extended reasoning before answering. Example: OpenAI's o-series, DeepSeek-R1, Claude's extended-thinking mode, and Gemini's Deep Think mode all pause to reason before giving a final answer.

Part 3 · Tokens & Cost

What You're Actually Paying For

Every prompt you send (input tokens) and every reply you receive (output tokens) are counted and billed separately — output usually costs more per token than input. Rule of thumb for English: roughly 4 characters, or about ¾ of a word, per token.

Worked example

A 300-word status update in ≈ 400 input tokens. A well-formed reply out ≈ 600 output tokens. On a model priced at $2 / $12 per million tokens: (400 × $2 + 600 × $12) ÷ 1,000,000 ≈ $0.008 — under a cent for that one exchange. Do it 10,000 times a month, and that's roughly $80.

Spending it well

Making the Bill Smaller

  • Right-size the model — match model tier to task difficulty.
  • Trim the context — send only the relevant paragraphs, not the whole manual.
  • Cache repeated instructions — a reused system prompt can cost a fraction on repeat calls.
  • Batch what isn't urgent — non-real-time jobs are often discounted around 50%.
  • Cap output length — ask for the summary, not the full transcript.
Example

Routing a bulk job — "tag these 10,000 support tickets by category" — to a budget-tier model instead of the flagship model can cut that bill by 20–25× for a task simple enough that the extra reasoning power adds nothing.

Part 4 · Prompting 101

What Is a Prompt?

Everything before this point explains the machine. This is where you learn to steer it.

A prompt is the input or instruction given to an AI system to elicit a response — a question, a command, or context plus instructions.

Question

"What is the capital of France?"

Command

"Write a summary of the attached text."

Context + instruction

"Based on the following data, explain the trends in a concise manner."

Trained on patterns, not meaning

How an LLM "Understands" Your Prompt

It doesn't understand meaning the way a person does. During training, it read billions of examples where a clear question or instruction was followed by a clear, well-formed answer — and it learned that shape: instruction in, helpful response out. Prompting works precisely because it triggers that learned pattern.

What training looked like

Q: What is the capital of France? A: Paris. Instruction: Summarize this email in two sentences. Response: ...

Millions of pairs shaped like this — textbooks, help forums, documentation, and human-written instruction/response examples — are what taught the model "a clear ask deserves a clear answer."

Write your prompt in a shape the model was rewarded for completing well, and it obliges. Write something vague, and it falls back to the most average, generic answer it has ever seen.

Vague — no clear pattern to match

Prompt: Explain how blockchain works.

Blockchain is a decentralized database that uses cryptography to secure transactions and maintain a distributed ledger.

Precise — matches a trained pattern

Prompt: You are a blockchain developer. Explain how blockchain works to someone with no technical background.

Blockchain is like a digital notebook shared across many computers. If you change something, everyone else's notebook updates too — so the record stays accurate and secure.

The cheapest lever you have

Why Prompting Is So Effective

Same model, same cost — wildly different quality of output, purely from how the instruction is phrased. No retraining, no data-science team, no procurement cycle. Just better words, today.

Without context

"Suggest a growth strategy."

With context

"Our retail bank has seen a 15% decline in customer acquisition this year. Two competitors recently launched AI-powered advisory products. The marketing budget stays unchanged. Suggest a growth strategy."

The name, explained

Why It's Called Prompt Engineering

Because a prompt that reliably works is designed and tested, not typed once and hoped for — the same discipline as engineering anything else: clear requirements, defined constraints, and a specified output. That's exactly what the next section, RTCCF, turns into a repeatable formula.

Part 5 · The RTCCF Framework

The Anatomy of a Good Prompt

Understanding why prompting works is one thing. Having a repeatable formula for it is another.
Role→ Task→ Context→ Constraint→ Format

Five elements, in order. Miss one, and the AI fills the gap with its own generic assumption.

R · Role

Role Play — Giving the AI an Identity

Assigning a role tells the model whose expertise, tone, and perspective to emulate. Nowhere is the effect more visible than in creative writing.

Without role play

Prompt: "Write a song about the separation of two lovers."

A plain, competent set of lyrics — technically correct, forgettable.

With role play

Prompt: "Imagine you are Javed Akhtar, the legendary poet and lyricist known for profound, poetic songwriting. Write a song on this situation about the separation of two lovers, using poetic imagery and deep metaphors."

"शाम का वक्त है, सूरज धीरे-धीरे पहाड़ियों के पीछे छुप रहा है... उसका दिल भारी हो गया है — वह जानता है, यह उनकी आख़िरी मुलाक़ात है।"

AI-generated sample, for illustration — same request, one identity assigned.

R · Role, in your world

Role Play, Solving Real Problems

Same framework, three real problems. Copy the prompt, swap in your own details.

HR

Problem

Two team members are in open conflict, dragging down morale — and you need to mediate without sounding like you're taking sides.

Role-play prompt 
You are a senior HR Business Partner with 15 years of experience mediating workplace conflict.

Two of my team members are in an ongoing disagreement: [describe the situation in 2-3 sentences]. It's starting to affect team morale.

Help me plan the mediation:
1. What should I ask each person privately, before any joint conversation?
2. How should I structure the joint conversation itself?
3. What does a fair, mutually acceptable resolution look like here?

Be direct if you think I'm about to take a side without realizing it.
What you get

A mediation plan shaped by someone who's actually run this before — including the blind spot you didn't know you had.

Sales

Problem

Regional sales dropped 18% this quarter, and leadership wants the real reason before the board meeting — not a guess.

Role-play prompt 
You are a senior sales operations analyst who specializes in root-cause revenue analysis.

Here is our sales data for the last 4 quarters, by region, rep, and product line:
[paste your data or attach the file]

1. Identify the 2-3 most likely causes of this quarter's 18% decline, backed by specific numbers from the data — not general sales advice.
2. For each cause, walk me through the causal chain: "because X happened, Y followed, which caused Z."
3. Flag anything that looks like a data anomaly rather than a real trend.
What you get

A numbers-backed causal story — "Region B lost 3 enterprise accounts, average deal size dropped 22%, which alone explains 11 of the 18 points" — not a generic "focus on retention" slide.

Marketing

Problem

You need a Gen Z product launch pitch, and the exec team will shred anything that sounds like generic marketing-speak.

Role-play prompt 
You are a performance marketing lead who has launched five Gen Z-focused consumer products in the last three years.

Pitch a go-to-market campaign for [product name/description]:
1. Platform choice and creative direction
2. The 3 KPIs you'd actually be judged on
3. Where you'd spend more, where you'd spend less, and why

Push back if my channel assumptions are wrong for this audience — don't just agree with me.
What you get

A pitch with real trade-offs and a point of view — not a template that could apply to any product.

T · Task   /   C · Context

Tell It the Job, Then Give It the Background

The real problem

In the real Sales Data.csv you'll use later today, revenue peaked at $3.39M in April 2019 and fell to $2.10M by September — a 38% drop — and leadership wants to know why before quarterly planning.

Task alone — no background

Prompt: "Why did our sales drop, and what should we do about it?"

A generic list — run a promotion, boost marketing spend, check inventory — that never once touches the actual data, because it was never given any.

Task + Context together

Prompt: "Analyze why revenue fell from $3.39M in April to $2.10M in September, and determine whether the decline is concentrated in specific cities or products, or broad-based. Sales Data.csv covers ~186,000 orders across 9 cities and 19 products: April had 18,279 orders averaging $185 each; September had 11,621 orders averaging $180 each."

Average order value barely moved — the drop is almost entirely fewer orders, spread evenly across every city and product. That's a volume problem, not a product or market problem.

Copy-paste prompt template 
[TASK] Analyze why [metric] changed by [amount] between [period A] and [period B], and determine whether the change is concentrated in specific segments or broad-based across all of them.

[CONTEXT] [Give the specific numbers your data actually shows for each period — counts, averages, top segments — not just the headline change.]
C · Constraint   /   F · Format

Draw the Boundaries, Then Shape the Answer

Constraint

The boundaries the answer must respect — a budget, a policy, a rule it isn't allowed to break, or information it isn't allowed to invent.

Format

The shape the answer must take — exact columns, a specific structure, nothing extra before or after it. The difference between an answer you have to read and one you can act on immediately.

Format — Compare AI Providers

Problem

You want a live, trustworthy comparison of what GPT, Claude, and Gemini actually cost right now — not whatever a blog post claimed six months ago.

Prompt 
Visit the three pages below and extract information about the latest flagship model from each provider.

- OpenAI Models: https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/models
- Anthropic Claude Pricing: https://www.anthropic.com/pricing
- Google Gemini API Pricing: https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/pricing

Create a comparison table with exactly these columns:
Provider | Model | Context Window | Input Price / 1M Tokens | Output Price / 1M Tokens | Key Capability

Rules:
- One row per provider.
- Use information only from the provided pages.
- Do not infer missing information.
- If information is unavailable, write N/A.
- Normalize all prices to USD per 1M tokens.
- Return only the table.
- Do not add explanations before or after the table.
What you get

A six-column table sourced live from each provider's own page — no invented numbers, no stale blog figures, and nothing to read before or after it.

Constraint — Banking

Problem

A small-business customer has missed two payments. Any restructuring option that isn't compliant just wastes the relationship manager's time.

Prompt 
Recommend 2-3 loan restructuring options for a small-business customer who has missed two consecutive payments.

Outstanding balance: $85,000. Original term: 5 years, 18 months remaining. Missed payments are due to a seasonal cash-flow gap — revenue is up 12% year-over-year, this isn't insolvency.

[CONSTRAINT] Every option must: stay within our 7-year maximum term, carry no more than $500 in restructuring fees, and comply with our forbearance policy (max 2 deferrals per 12 months).

Present each option with: new monthly payment, total interest impact, and a compliance check against each constraint above.
What you get

Every option is already inside the guardrails a relationship manager has to work within — nothing to discard because it's not compliant.

All five together

One Prompt, Fully Engineered

One real decision — build or buy an AI support assistant — carrying all five parts of RTCCF at once.

Act as a Chief Technology Officer with experience evaluating build-vs-buy decisions for customer-facing AI tools.

Evaluate whether we should build our own AI customer support assistant or purchase a commercial solution.

We receive approximately 50,000 support requests a month. Average response time is currently 48 hours, and customer satisfaction has declined 12% over the past two quarters.

Budget is capped at $300,000. The solution must be implemented within 6 months and comply with our data-privacy regulations.

Present your answer as a comparison table — Option, Estimated Cost, Benefits, Risks, Time to Implement, Recommendation — followed by a 3-sentence executive summary.

Each paragraph above maps to one block, in order — that's the whole formula, written as a single prompt you could paste right now.

Part 6 · RAG

The Problem: A Closed-Book Exam

RTCCF makes the AI answer better. This is what makes it answer correctly — with your own information.

An LLM's knowledge freezes at training time. It has never seen your HR policy, your product manual, or this quarter's numbers — and when it doesn't know, it can still answer confidently. That is a closed-book exam with no "I don't know" option.

Retrieval-Augmented Generation

Turning It Into an Open-Book Exam

Step 1 of 5 — Your question
"How many casual leaves do I get?"
Searching: HR_Policy_Manual.pdf …

The RAG Prompt, Fully Engineered

The same open-book-exam prompt from the last slide — written properly, with all five RTCCF parts doing their job.

You are a meticulous document-analysis assistant. You answer strictly from the provided source material — never from general knowledge or memory.

Answer the question below using only the attached document, and cite the exact passage(s) you used to answer it.

Document: [attach HR_Policy_Manual.pdf]
Question: How many casual leaves do I get?

If the document does not contain the information needed to answer this question, respond only with: "Insufficient information." Every answer you do give must carry a citation — never answer without one.

Cite each passage in this format: {"citation": "...", "page": page_number, "paragraph": paragraph_number}
