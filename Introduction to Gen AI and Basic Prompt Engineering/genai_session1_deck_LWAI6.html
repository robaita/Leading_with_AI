What Is Generative AI?

Generative AI is AI that creates new content — text, images, audio, video, code — instead of only analysing or labelling what already exists.

Predictive AI

Is this email spam? Will this customer churn?
Output: a label or a number.

Generative AI

Write the reply. Draft the ad. Summarise the contract.
Output: new content.

Today we zoom into the text branch — the LLM — because it is the engine inside most of the tools you’ll use.

Part 1 · Understand Generative AI

What Is a Language Model?

Behind every chatbot, one simple idea does almost all of the work.

A language model is a system trained to predict the next most likely word (token), given everything that came before it. That is the entire trick — repeated, extremely well, at enormous scale.

Example

Your phone's keyboard already does this: type "I am currently..." and it suggests "at the gym." A language model does the same thing — it has simply read far more text before making that guess.

Game · be the language model

The Next-Word Game

Guess the blank. Then see what a model would predict — and how sure it is.

Probabilities are illustrative — they show the typical shape of a real model’s next-token distribution.

The "L" in LLM

What Does "Large" Actually Mean?

Scale, across three dimensions at once:

Parameters

The adjustable internal "dials" the model tunes during training — modern models have tens to hundreds of billions of them.

Training data

Trillions of words, drawn from books, code, articles, and the public web — far more than any one person could read in several lifetimes.

Compute

Specialised chips (GPUs) running for weeks or months to find the right value for every one of those parameters.

Example

If a smaller, task-specific model is like an employee trained only on your company's manual, a "large" model is like a new hire who has already read every manual, forum post, and news article on the internet — broad, but still needing your specific instructions to be useful to you.

Part 1 · The evolution of LLMs

The Journey of LLMs — Major Milestones

2017

The Transformer

Google’s “Attention Is All You Need”: a model can weigh every word against every other at once.

2018

GPT-1 & BERT

Pre-train on huge amounts of text, then adapt. The “pre-trained model” idea takes hold.

2019

GPT-2

1.5 billion parameters and fluent paragraphs — and the first “is this too risky to release?” debate.

2020

GPT-3

175 billion parameters. Show it a few examples in the prompt and it learns the task — no retraining.

Nov 2022

ChatGPT

A plain chat box, tuned to follow instructions. About 100 million users in roughly two months.

2023

The race opens

GPT-4, Claude, Gemini — and Meta’s Llama makes open-weights models mainstream. Models start to see images.

2024

Multimodal & reasoning

Native voice and vision, million-token context, and “thinking” models that reason before they answer.

2025 → now

The agent era

Open reasoning models cut costs; models use tools, write code and work for hours. Price per token keeps falling.

Dial the Randomness: Temperature

The model doesn’t pick the top word every time — it samples from the probabilities. Temperature reshapes them.

A closer look

Tokenization

Tokenization is the process of breaking text into the small chunks — tokens — that a model actually reads and generates. A token might be a whole word, a piece of a word, or even just a punctuation mark; words are rarely kept whole.

We need it because a neural network can't read letters or words directly — it only works with numbers. Tokenization is the translation step: it chops text into a fixed vocabulary of pieces, and every piece maps to a number the model can compute with. It's also directly why you're billed per token, not per word.

Example
unbelievable

"Unbelievable" becomes three pieces, not one. Uncommon words split into odd fragments — which is exactly why a model can sometimes stumble counting the letters in a word like "strawberry": it isn't reading letter by letter, it's reading in these puzzle-piece chunks.

Try it — paste these into a tokenizer and compare the counts: Hyderabad · हैदराबाद · 😀 · def hello(): · 1234567. More tokens means more cost.

Not all LLMs are the same

Types of LLMs

Causal / autoregressive

Predicts strictly left-to-right, never peeking at words that come later. Where it applies: nearly every chat model in use today — GPT, Claude, Gemini, Qwen, and Llama are all built this way underneath.

Dense model

Every parameter activates for every request. Example: Meta's Llama 3, Mistral 7B, and the original GPT-3 all run their full network on every single prompt, no shortcuts taken.

Mixture-of-Experts

Only relevant "expert" sub-networks switch on per request. Example: Mistral's Mixtral, DeepSeek-V3, and Qwen's MoE models route this way explicitly; GPT-4 and Gemini are widely reported to use a similar approach.

"Thinking" / reasoning model

Works through extended reasoning before answering. Example: OpenAI's o-series, DeepSeek-R1, Claude's extended-thinking mode, and Gemini's Deep Think mode all pause to reason before giving a final answer.

Part 4 · Tokens & Cost

What You’re Actually Paying For

Every prompt you send (input tokens) and every reply you receive (output tokens) are counted and billed separately — output usually costs more per token than input. Rule of thumb for English: roughly 4 characters, or about ¾ of a word, per token.

Subscription

Chat apps

A flat ~$20/month for access, with fair-use limits. You pay for the seat, not the token.

API

Things you build

Pay per token, per call. Cheap per request, but it scales with volume — this is where bills surprise people.

Enterprise

Company-wide

Seats plus usage, with data controls and admin. You are paying for governance as well as tokens.

Hidden costs

Chat history is re-sent as input on every turn, so long chats get pricier. “Thinking” models also bill their hidden reasoning as output tokens.

Worked example

A 300-word status update in ≈ 400 input tokens. A well-formed reply out ≈ 600 output tokens. On a model priced at $2 / $12 per million tokens: (400 × $2 + 600 × $12) ÷ 1,000,000 ≈ $0.008 — under a cent for that one exchange. Do it 10,000 times a month, and that’s roughly $80.

Spending it well

Making the Bill Smaller

  • Right-size the model — match model tier to task difficulty.
  • Trim the context — send only the relevant paragraphs, not the whole manual.
  • Cache repeated instructions — a reused system prompt can cost a fraction on repeat calls.
  • Batch what isn't urgent — non-real-time jobs are often discounted around 50%.
  • Cap output length — ask for the summary, not the full transcript.
Example

Routing a bulk job — "tag these 10,000 support tickets by category" — to a budget-tier model instead of the flagship model can cut that bill by 20–25× for a task simple enough that the extra reasoning power adds nothing.

Take-home · Session 1 cheat sheet

Generative AI & LLMs — One Page

1 · What it is
  • GenAI creates new content: text, image, audio, video, code.
  • An LLM predicts the next token, over and over.
  • Plausible ≠ true. Verify facts, numbers and citations.
2 · Tokens
  • 1 token ≈ 4 characters ≈ ¾ of a word.
  • 1,000 words ≈ 1,300 tokens. Hindi and code use more.
  • Context window = how much the model holds in mind per chat.
3 · Temperature
  • 0 focused & repeatable: facts, extraction, code.
  • 0.7–1 balanced: everyday writing.
  • >1 creative & risky: brainstorming.
  • Apps hide it; AI Studio / APIs expose it.
4 · Pick a model
  • Daily drafting → mid tier.
  • Hard reasoning → flagship / thinking.
  • Bulk tagging → budget tier.
  • Huge files → long context. Private → open weights.
  • Check LMArena + Artificial Analysis.
5 · Pick a platform
  • ChatGPT: all-rounder, voice, images.
  • Gemini: Google apps, long context, video.
  • Claude: writing, coding, documents.
  • Pick the task, then test two.
6 · Estimate the cost
  • cost = (in × in-price + out × out-price) ÷ 1,000,000
  • Output costs more than input.
  • Chat history is re-sent every turn.
7 · Cut the bill
  • Right-size the model.
  • Trim the context.
  • Cache repeated instructions.
  • Batch what isn’t urgent.
  • Cap output length.
8 · Stay safe
  • Don’t paste confidential data into public tools.
  • You own the output — review it before it ships.
