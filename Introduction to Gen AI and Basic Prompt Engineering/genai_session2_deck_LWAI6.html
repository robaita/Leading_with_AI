Part 1 · Prompts, from the ground up

What Is a Prompt?

Think of the model as a brilliant new hire on day one — and your prompt as the brief you hand them.

A prompt is the input you give an AI system to get a response. It can be a question, a command, or context plus instructions — even a few examples.

Question

“What is the capital of France?”

Command

“Write a summary of the attached text.”

Context + instruction

“Based on the following sales data, explain the trends in three bullet points.”

Instruction + examples

“Label each review. ‘Loved it’ → Positive. ‘Too slow’ → Negative. ‘Fast delivery’ → ?”

The name, explained

Why It’s Called Prompt Engineering

Because a prompt that reliably works is designed and tested, not typed once and hoped for — the same discipline as engineering anything else.

Write→Run→Inspect→Refine↺
01

Clear requirements

You say exactly what you need, and for whom.

02

Defined constraints

You set the limits: length, tone, rules, sources.

03

A specified output

You decide the shape of the answer before it’s written.

Why it matters

Same model, same cost — wildly different quality, purely from how the instruction is written. No retraining, no data team: just better words, today.

Try it · 3 minutes

Vague vs Sharp

Open ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini. Run each prompt in a new chat, and compare the two answers.

Prompt A — vague
Write a message about the meeting.
Prompt B — sharper
Write a 4-line WhatsApp message to my team of 8. Remind them that Thursday's 3 pm review has moved to Friday 11 am, room B. Friendly but professional. End by asking everyone to confirm attendance.
What to notice

Which one could you send without editing? Look at what changed between A and B: audience · details · length · tone · the ask. That gap is what prompt engineering closes.

Part 2 · Why prompts work

How Supervised Fine-Tuning Teaches a Model to Follow Instructions

1 · StartA base model that only completes text
→
2 · Show examplesThousands of instruction → ideal response pairs, written by people
→
3 · TrainIt learns to write the response that follows each instruction
→
4 · ResultAn assistant that follows instructions
One training example
Instruction — what to doSummarise the email below in one line.
Input — the material“Hi team, my desk was promised by 10 Oct, then 15 Oct, and still hasn’t arrived. I want a refund if it isn’t here by Saturday.”
Output — the ideal responseRohan will ask for a full refund if his desk doesn’t arrive by Saturday.

The model is trained to predict the output, word by word, given the instruction and input. After tens of thousands of examples, it learns the pattern: instruction in → matching answer out.

The same example, written two ways for training 
Below is an instruction that describes a task,
paired with an input that provides more context.
Write a response that completes the request.

### Instruction:
Summarise the email below in one line.

### Input:
Hi team, my desk was promised by 10 Oct…

### Response:
Rohan will ask for a full refund if his desk
doesn’t arrive by Saturday.

Used by Stanford Alpaca (2023): about 52,000 instruction examples turned a plain LLaMA 7B base model into an instruction follower. Records are stored as instruction, input, output.

<|im_start|>system
You are a helpful assistant.<|im_end|>
<|im_start|>user
Summarise the email below in one line.
Hi team, my desk was promised by 10 Oct…<|im_end|>
<|im_start|>assistant
Rohan will ask for a full refund if his desk
doesn’t arrive by Saturday.<|im_end|>

Used by many chat models: each message carries a role (system, user, assistant). The model is trained on the highlighted assistant part — and this is the same shape your prompts take today.

Why this matters for you

The model was trained on text shaped like instruction → response, so a clear instruction matches exactly what it was rewarded for. The more your prompt looks like a good instruction, the better it aligns. (A later stage, learning from human feedback, then tunes tone, safety and fairness.)

The Anatomy of a Good Prompt

You wouldn’t hand a new hire a one-line sticky note. You’d brief them. A good brief has five parts.
Role→Task→Context→Constraint→Format
R

Role

Who should it be?

“Imagine you are Javed Akhtar…”
T

Task

What exactly is the job?

“Write the mukhda of a romantic song…”
C

Context

What background does it need?

“A 1980s Lucknow love story; a rainy farewell…”
C

Constraint

What rules and limits apply?

“4 lines, AABB, Hindi only.”
F

Format

What should the output look like?

“Devanagari, Roman and English meaning.”

One running example — a lyricist writing a song for a new Bollywood film — plus a business example for each element. Miss one part, and the AI fills the gap with its own generic assumption.

R · Role · 1 of 2

Role — Giving the AI an Identity

A role tells the model whose expertise, vocabulary and point of view to use. Nowhere is it easier to see than in poetry.

Without a role

Prompt: “Write a song about the separation of two lovers.”

तुम बिन अकेला हूँ मैं, आँसू बहाता हूँ मैं, लौट आओ तुम, लौट आओ, दिल ये पुकारे बार-बार…

With a role

Prompt: “Imagine you are Javed Akhtar, the legendary poet and lyricist known for profound, poetic songwriting. Write a song on the separation of two lovers, using poetic imagery and deep metaphors.”

शाम ढली तो परछाइयाँ लंबी हो गईं, कुछ बातें थीं जो होंठों तक आकर खो गईं। आख़िरी मोड़ पर रुककर तुमने जो देखा, वक़्त ने उस पल को तस्वीर बना दिया…

AI-generated samples for illustration, in the style of the role — not written by the person named.

Why it works: during training the model read how many kinds of writers write. A role doesn’t add facts — it picks which voice to use: imagery, restraint, metaphor.

R · Role · 2 of 2

Role — Make It Write Like You

A role can also be you. Describe your job, tone and style, and the model writes emails that sound like you, not like a template.

Without a role

Prompt: “Write an email telling my client Priya that the Q3 report will be two days late.”

Subject: Delay in Q3 Report Dear Priya, I hope this email finds you well. I am writing to inform you that, due to unforeseen circumstances, the Q3 report will be delayed by two days. We apologise for any inconvenience and appreciate your understanding. Best regards

With a role, tone and style

Prompt: “You are Meera Rao, Head of Operations at a logistics company. Voice: warm but direct; short sentences; plain English; one light touch of humour; no corporate buzzwords. Sign off ‘Warm regards, Meera’. Write an email telling my client Priya that the Q3 report will be two days late.”

Subject: Q3 report — new date is Friday Hi Priya, Quick heads-up: the Q3 report will reach you on Friday, not Wednesday. I’d rather give you a real date than a hopeful one. I’ll send the draft numbers tonight so you can plan around them. Thanks for your patience. Warm regards, Meera

The levers inside a role: job title · tone · style · formality · sign-off · phrases to avoid. Even better: paste two of your own emails and say “match this voice.”

Try it · 4 minutes
You are [your name], [your job] at [your company].
Voice: [warm / formal / crisp]. Style: [short sentences, no jargon, ...].
Sign off: [your usual closing].

Write an email to [person] about [topic].

Run it once, then add “match the voice of the two emails below” and paste two of yours. Compare.

T · Task

Task — Why Precision Matters

The task is the specific job to do. A vague task gets a vague result — and the model can’t read your mind. Precision means an action verb + the exact deliverable + its purpose.

Vague task

Prompt: “Write something for my movie.”

Sure! I’d be happy to help. Here are some ideas: a poem, a dialogue, a script outline or a song. What would you like? Let me know the genre, length and style and I’ll get started!

Precise task

Prompt: “Write the mukhda — the opening four lines — of a romantic duet for my new Bollywood film.”

तेरे बिना ये शहर अधूरा लगता है, तू साथ हो तो हर लम्हा पूरा लगता है। ये बारिशें भी तेरा नाम दोहराती हैं, हवा भी तेरा ही पैग़ाम सुनाती है।

Everyday version · vague“Make me dinner.” → “Sure! How about pasta?”
Everyday version · precise“Plan a 3-course vegetarian dinner for 6 guests, under ₹2,000, ready in 90 minutes.” → Starter, main and dessert, with costs.

AI-generated samples for illustration, in the style of the role — not written by the person named.

The gap: same model, same minute. One answer asks questions back; the other delivers the exact piece you needed. Say what, how much and what for.

Try it · 3 minutes
Rewrite this vague task into a precise one (verb + deliverable + purpose):

"Help me with my presentation."

Run both versions and compare.

Does your version say what to produce and what it is for?

C · Context · 1 of 2

Context — The Story Behind the Song

Context is the background the model can’t know. For a film song, the plot, the character and the scene it plays in decide every word.

The context we addFilm: a 1980s love story set in Lucknow. Character: Zoya, a shy librarian who writes letters she never posts. Situation: the song plays on a rainy morning as she finally mails her first letter, while Arjun’s train pulls out of the station.
Role + task, no context

Prompt: “Imagine you are Javed Akhtar… Write the mukhda of a romantic song for my new film.”

शाम ढली तो परछाइयाँ लंबी हो गईं, कुछ बातें थीं जो होंठों तक आकर खो गईं। तेरे बिना ये रास्ते कुछ और ही लगने लगे, हर मोड़ पर हम ख़ुद को ही ढूँढने लगे।

Role + task + context

Prompt: “Imagine you are Javed Akhtar… Write the mukhda of a romantic song for my new film. Film: a 1980s love story in Lucknow. Zoya is a shy librarian who writes letters she never posts. The song plays as she finally mails her first letter on a rainy morning while Arjun’s train leaves.”

भीगे लिफ़ाफ़े में रख दी है दिल की बात, जो कह न सकी ज़ुबाँ, वो कह गई बरसात। सीटी की गूँज में खोया है साथ, पटरी पे रह गई बस इक मुलाक़ात।

AI-generated samples for illustration, in the style of the role — not written by the person named.

Why it works: the first song could be in any film. The second could only be in this one — letters, rain, the platform. Context turns generic into specific.

C · Context · 2 of 2

Context — The Same Idea in Sales

In business, context is what turns a generic message into one the client actually reads: who they are, what they said, and what happens next.

Without context

Prompt: “Write a follow-up email after our product demo.”

Hi, Thank you for taking the time to attend our demo. Please let us know if you have any questions or would like to schedule a follow-up. Best regards

With context

Prompt: “Write a follow-up email after our product demo. Context: the client is Nisha Verma, COO of a 200-store pharmacy chain. In the demo she worried about integration with their old billing system and asked about pricing for 200 stores. Our pilot takes 3 weeks. She decides by month-end.”

Hi Nisha, Thanks for the sharp questions yesterday — especially on the billing system. We’ve integrated with similar legacy setups; I’ve attached a one-page note on how. For 200 stores I’ll send a tiered quote by Thursday, and a 3-week pilot in two stores would let you test everything before your month-end decision. Would Tuesday work for a 20-minute call?

Context checklist: who is it for · what happened · which facts and numbers · what is the goal · what happens next.

Try it · 4 minutes
Take a real message you need to write this week.
Version 1: only the task.
Version 2: add four facts the AI could not possibly know (who, what happened, numbers, goal).

Run both.

Notice how the answer stops being generic and starts sounding like your situation.

C · Constraint · 1 of 2

Constraint — How the Song Is Built

Constraints are the rules the answer must respect. For a song: its structure, rhyme, length, language — and what to avoid.

Mukhda · 4 lines · AABB→Antara 1 · 6 lines→Antara 2 · 6 lines→Hook repeats
Without constraints

Prompt: “… [role, task and context included]”

भीगे लिफ़ाफ़े में रख दी है दिल की बात, जो कह न सकी ज़ुबाँ, वो कह गई बरसात। सीटी की गूँज में खोया है साथ, पटरी पे रह गई बस इक मुलाक़ात। प्यार की ये चिट्ठी, ज़िंदगी भर का साथ, I will wait for you in this rain tonight। (6 lines · an English line · ‘dil’, ‘pyaar’, ‘zindagi’)

With constraints

Prompt: “… Constraints: exactly 4 lines, rhyme scheme AABB, Hindi only with no English words, and avoid clichés like ‘dil’, ‘zindagi’ and ‘pyaar’.”

भीगे लिफ़ाफ़े में रख दी है इक बात, जो कह न सकी ज़ुबाँ, वो कह गई बरसात। सीटी की गूँज में खोया है साथ, पटरी पे रह गई बस इक मुलाक़ात। (4 lines · AABB · Hindi only · no clichés)

AI-generated samples for illustration, in the style of the role — not written by the person named.

Why it works: a good song has a shape. Constraints give the model the shape, the limits and the “don’ts” a lyricist would carry in their head.

C · Constraint · 2 of 2

Constraint — The Same Idea in HR

In business, constraints protect you: from rambling, from risky claims, from broken policy. Here is an interview rejection email.

Without constraints

Prompt: “Write an email rejecting a candidate after an interview.”

Dear Rahul, Thank you for interviewing with us. Unfortunately we found your communication skills lacking and you seemed too junior for the role. We will keep your CV on file and contact you when a suitable position opens up in the future. (Gives a reason · promises future roles · too long)

With constraints

Prompt: “Write an email rejecting a candidate after an interview. Constraints: under 90 words; warm and respectful; give no reason beyond ‘we chose a candidate whose experience matched more closely’; do not promise future roles; offer feedback only if they ask.”

Dear Rahul, Thank you for the time you invested in interviewing with us. After careful consideration, we’ve decided to move forward with a candidate whose experience matched this role more closely. We appreciated your thoughtful answers and wish you every success. If you’d like feedback, just reply to this email. Warm regards, Anita

Constraint ideas: length · tone · what to avoid · “use only the information I give you” · “if you don’t know, say so” · policy rules.

Try it · 3 minutes
Take your last prompt and add three constraints:
- a length limit
- a tone (e.g. "warm, no jargon")
- one thing it must NOT do

Run it again.

Constraints protect you from rambling, risky claims and invented facts.

Format — From a Web Search to a Table

Format shines when the model searches the web for you and you need the findings in a shape you can use straight away.

Search the web for the 3 most popular electric scooters in India right now.
Return only a table with these columns:
Model | Price (₹ lakh, ex-showroom) | Claimed range (km) | One-line verdict | Source URL

Use only information from the pages you found. Write N/A if something is missing.
No text before or after the table.

Several electric scooters are popular in India right now. Many buyers like Model A for its range and price, while Model B is known for its features and Model C for build quality. Prices vary by city and dealer, so it’s best to check each manufacturer’s website for the latest details…

Part 5 · RAG

The Problem: A Closed-Book Exam

RTCCF makes the AI answer better. RAG makes it answer correctly — with your own information.

An LLM’s knowledge freezes at training time. It has never seen your HR policy, your product manual or this quarter’s numbers — and when it doesn’t know, it can still answer confidently.

Frozen knowledge

It knows nothing after its training cut-off.

No access to your files

Your policies, contracts and data were never in the library.

Confident guessing

A closed-book exam with no “I don’t know” option.

Asking without the document
You: “How many casual leave days do I get at my company?”
AI: “Most companies offer 8–10 casual leave days a year, so you likely get around 10.”

Sounds confident. But it has never read your policy — it is guessing.

Retrieval-Augmented Generation

Turning It Into an Open-Book Exam

Step 1 of 6

1 · Your documents — the HR policy, a manual, last quarter’s report.

chunk 1Leave policychunk 2Casual leave: 12 dayschunk 3Travel claimschunk 4Work from homechunk 5Notice period

2 · Cut into small chunks and filed in a searchable index — like sticky tabs in a book.

“How many casual leaves do I get?”

3 · You ask a question.

chunk 1Leave policychunk 2 · best matchCasual leave: 12 dayschunk 3Travel claimschunk 4Work from home

4 · Retrieve the chunks that best match the question — she opens the book at the right page.

The prompt the model actually receivesAnswer the question using only this passage: “Section 4.2 — Employees are entitled to 12 casual leave days per calendar year.”
Question: How many casual leaves do I get?

5 · Augment the prompt — your question and the retrieved text travel together.

“You’re entitled to 12 casual leave days per year. [Source: HR Policy Manual, Section 4.2]”
Not in the document? “Insufficient information — this isn’t covered in the provided document.”

6 · Generate a grounded answer, with a citation — or an honest “I can’t find it”.

RTCCF meets RAG

The RAG Prompt, Fully Engineered

The open-book-exam prompt, written properly — all five RTCCF parts doing their job.

You are a meticulous document-analysis assistant. You answer strictly from the provided source material, never from general knowledge or memory.

Answer the question below using only the attached document, and cite the exact passage you used.

Document: [attach HR_Policy_Manual.pdf]
Question: How many casual leaves do I get?

If the document does not contain the information needed, reply only with: "Insufficient information." Never answer without a citation.

Cite each passage as: {"citation": "...", "page": page_number, "paragraph": paragraph_number}
Role→Task→Context→Constraint→Format

The constraint forces an honest “insufficient information” instead of a guess. The format makes the citation something you can check — or something an app can read.

Try it · 6 minutes

Try RAG on Your Own Document

  1. Upload a document you’re allowed to share — a policy, a manual or a report (no confidential data).
  2. Ask something that is in it. Check the answer and the citation.
  3. Ask something that is NOT in it. A good setup says so instead of guessing.
  4. Compare: ask the first question in a fresh chat with no document.
Starter prompt
Answer using only the attached document. Cite the section or page you used. If the document does not contain the answer, reply exactly: "Not found in the document."

Question: [your question]

Why it matters

Accuracy, Trust, and No Guessing

Accuracy & trust

Answers are grounded in your actual documents, not a plausible-sounding guess.

Citations

Every answer can point back to its exact source.

No silent hallucination

When it’s not in the document, the system says so.

Works on private data

No retraining needed — your documents are looked up, not baked into the model.

Prompt Engineering — One Page

1 · The idea
  • A prompt is the brief you give a smart, literal new hire.
  • Training taught the model to follow requests; your prompt steers it.
  • Vague in → generic out.
2 · RTCCF template
  • Role: You are a…
  • Task: Write / compare / classify…
  • Context: audience, facts, goal
  • Constraints: length, tone, never…
  • Format: bullets, table, headings
3 · Role & Task
  • “You are an experienced [job] who [speciality].”
  • “Explain it to a [audience].”
  • Start with a verb. One job per prompt.
4 · Context checklist
  • Who is it for?
  • What’s the situation?
  • Which facts and numbers?
  • What was already tried?
  • What is the goal?
5 · Constraints & format
  • Length · tone · what to avoid.
  • “Use only the information I give you.”
  • “If you don’t know, say so.”
  • Ask for a table, bullets or headings — and name them.
6 · Iterate, don’t restart
  • Write → Run → Inspect → Refine.
  • Reply with a tweak: “shorter”, “add risks”, “for a CFO”.
  • Save prompts that work.
7 · Many jobs, one model
  • Summarise · classify · extract · translate · draft · analyse · code · brainstorm.
  • Add 2–3 examples to teach a pattern.
8 · RAG prompt
  • “Answer only from the attached document.”
  • “Cite the section or page.”
  • “If it isn’t there, say ‘Not found’.”
9 · Stay safe
  • No confidential data in public tools.
  • Tuning reduces bias and errors; it doesn’t remove them — review what matters.
