Part A · Advanced Prompting

Beyond RTCCF

RTCCF gets you a good prompt. These six techniques are what you reach for when a good prompt still isn’t enough.

Same rule every time: what it is, then one real example — and a version you can run on your own data.

1 · Chain-of-Thought (CoT)

Make It Show Its Working

Ask the model to work step by step instead of jumping straight to an answer. Each step builds on the one before — so the reasoning becomes visible, and checkable, before you trust it.

Each step uses only the output of the step before it: a chain, not a leap. If the result looks wrong, you can see exactly which step broke.

Here is a document of about 500 words:
[paste your document here]

Work in three dependent steps. Show the output of each step before moving to the next.

Step 1 - Summarise the document in 150 words.
Step 2 - Take your Step 1 summary and translate it into Hindi.
Step 3 - Take your Step 2 Hindi text and give me the 2-3 most important points, as bullets of 2-3 words each.
Need a document to try it on? Open for a ready-made 500-word sample
Small businesses across India are quietly changing how they talk to customers. Where a shop owner once answered every WhatsApp message personally, many now use an AI assistant to handle the first reply. The shift began when orders moved online faster than teams could grow. Owners found themselves answering the same ten questions all day: prices, delivery times, return rules, store hours. AI assistants, trained on a short list of frequently asked questions, can answer these in seconds, at any hour, in several languages. For a team of three, that can mean getting back two or three hours every day.

The benefits are not only about speed. Customers increasingly expect quick replies, and a question left unanswered for hours often becomes a lost sale. An assistant that responds immediately, politely and consistently can protect sales that would otherwise be lost. It also reduces errors. Human staff, when tired or rushed, may quote the wrong price or forget a policy. A well-configured assistant gives the same approved answer every time, and it can hand over to a person the moment a conversation becomes sensitive or complicated.

There are real risks, however. An assistant that is given incomplete information may guess, and a confident wrong answer can damage trust faster than a slow right one. Owners therefore need to decide clearly what the assistant may and may not do: it can share opening hours and delivery estimates, but it should not promise refunds or discuss anything involving payments without a human. Data is another concern. Customer messages often contain names, addresses and phone numbers, so businesses must understand where this information is stored and who can see it before they connect any tool to their chats.

Businesses that succeed with these tools tend to start small. They pick one channel, usually WhatsApp or the website chat, and write down the twenty questions they hear most often, with the exact answers they want customers to receive. They test the assistant themselves for a week, deliberately asking awkward questions, and note every wrong or awkward reply. Only then do they let it talk to real customers, and even then they review a sample of conversations each week, improving the instructions as they go.

The lesson is that AI assistants work best as a first responder, not as a replacement for people. Staff are freed from repetitive questions and can spend their time on the conversations that need judgment, empathy or negotiation. Owners who treat the assistant like a new team member, with clear instructions, regular feedback and defined limits, report calmer days and happier customers. Those who switch it on and walk away usually discover the problems from an upset customer. The technology is ready; the discipline of managing it is what separates good results from disappointing ones.
1 · Chain-of-Thought · a second example

Make It Show Its Working — Movie Reviews

Each stage sees only the stage before it. If the final verdict looks wrong, you can point to the stage that broke.

Analyze the sentiment of these five movie reviews by reasoning through five stages, showing your work at each one before moving to the next.

Reviews:
1. "The acting was phenomenal and the soundtrack was beautiful, but the pacing dragged in the second half."
2. "Visually stunning, but the plot felt predictable and the ending was rushed."
3. "A slow start, though the performances were captivating enough to keep me watching until the emotional final act."
4. "Every scene felt recycled from better films - I struggled to stay awake."
5. "A near-perfect blend of humor and heart; the lead's performance alone is worth the ticket."

Stage 1 - Extract the key aspects mentioned in each review (acting, plot, pacing, visuals, soundtrack, etc.).
Stage 2 - Using the aspects from Stage 1, assign a sentiment (positive, negative, or neutral) to each aspect.
Stage 3 - Using the aspect-level sentiments from Stage 2, determine the overall sentiment of each individual review.
Stage 4 - Using the review-level sentiments from Stage 3, calculate the distribution of positive, negative, mixed, and neutral reviews.
Stage 5 - Using the distribution from Stage 4, state the movie's overall sentiment and the evidence supporting that decision.

2 · Tree-of-Thought (ToT)

Explore Several Paths, Then Pick One

Instead of one reasoning path, the model explores several competing strategies in parallel, reasons through each independently, then compares them and picks the strongest.

Chain-of-Thought

One line of reasoning, followed deeper and deeper — good when there's basically one right way to work through a problem.

Tree-of-Thought

Several different lines of reasoning, explored side by side, then judged against each other — good when there are genuinely different strategies worth weighing.

A simple example · same movie, three competing strategies
Aspect-based

Extract acting, plot, visuals, pacing — score each, then aggregate up to an overall sentiment.

Overall-tone

Read each review holistically and classify it positive, negative, or mixed — no sub-scoring.

Critic vs. audience

Separate critic reviews from audience reviews, and compare whether the two populations agree.

Three independent verdicts, then one comparison step that says which strategy's conclusion to trust — and why the other two don't change the final answer.

2 · Tree-of-Thought · the prompt

Three Strategies, One Verdict

The prompt names the strategies, asks the model to reason through each independently, and only then compare them and pick one — explaining why the others lose.

Determine the overall sentiment of this movie using three independent strategies, then compare them.

Reviews:
1. "The acting was phenomenal and the soundtrack was beautiful, but the pacing dragged in the second half."
2. "Visually stunning, but the plot felt predictable and the ending was rushed."
3. "A slow start, though the performances were captivating enough to keep me watching until the emotional final act."

Explore these three strategies separately:
1. Aspect-based — extract the aspects mentioned (acting, plot, pacing, visuals, etc.), score each, then aggregate to an overall sentiment.
2. Overall-tone — read each review holistically and classify it directly as positive, negative, or mixed, with no sub-scoring.
3. Critic vs. audience — treat reviews 1 and 2 as critic reviews and review 3 as an audience review, and compare whether the two groups agree.

Reason through all three independently and show your work for each. Then compare their conclusions and give one final verdict on the movie's overall sentiment, explaining which strategy (or strategies) you trusted most and why.
Chain vs tree

Chain-of-Thought goes deeper along one path. Tree-of-Thought goes wider across several paths, then judges them — use it when there are genuinely different ways to solve the problem.

3 · One-shot Learning

Teach It With One Example

Show the model one worked example of the exact input → output pattern you want, then give it a new input. You teach by demonstration instead of description.

1 · ShowOne labelled example
→
2 · GiveA new review, unlabelled
→
3 · GetThe same labelling pattern
A simple example · movie review, one worked example
You are labeling movie reviews with aspect-level sentiment. Learn the pattern from this ONE example, then apply it to a new review.

Example review: "The acting was phenomenal and the soundtrack was beautiful, but the pacing dragged in the second half."
Labeled aspects:
- Acting: Positive
- Soundtrack: Positive
- Pacing: Negative

Now label this new review the same way:
"The visuals were stunning and the lead performance was captivating, though the plot felt predictable."
When one example is enough

When the pattern is simple and the model has seen similar tasks. If the output wobbles, add more examples — that’s few-shot, next.

3 · Few-shot Learning

Two Examples Beat One

Same task, but now with two worked examples. More examples show the pattern more clearly — the model sees what stays the same (the format) and what changes (the aspects).

1 · ShowTwo labelled examples
→
2 · GiveA new review, unlabelled
→
3 · GetA more reliable pattern
The same movie task · two worked examples
You are labeling movie reviews with aspect-level sentiment. Learn the pattern from these TWO examples, then apply it to a new review.

Example 1 review: "The acting was phenomenal and the soundtrack was beautiful, but the pacing dragged in the second half."
Labeled aspects:
- Acting: Positive
- Soundtrack: Positive
- Pacing: Negative

Example 2 review: "Visually stunning, but the plot felt predictable and the ending was rushed."
Labeled aspects:
- Visuals: Positive
- Plot: Negative
- Ending: Negative

Now label this new review the same way:
"A slow start, though the performances were captivating enough to keep me watching until the emotional final act."
One vs few

One-shot is quick. Few-shot (2–5 examples) is steadier when the format matters or the cases vary. Choose examples that cover the variety you expect.

3 · Few-shot — on your own data

Train It to Write Emails Like You

Give it three of your own emails as examples, let it learn your style, then ask it to write a new one. Swap the sample emails below for yours.

Prompt 1 · learn my style (Examples 1, 2 and 3)
Below are three emails I wrote. Study them and write a short STYLE PROFILE of how I write: tone, formality, sentence length, vocabulary, humour, structure, greeting and sign-off, and anything I always or never do. Keep the profile short enough that I can reuse it.

Example 1
Subject: Quick update on the vendor contract
Hi Rahul,
Short version: legal signed off yesterday. We're good to go.
Can you send the final invoice by Friday? I'd like to close this before the month ends.
Thanks a ton - you've made this painless.
Cheers,
Meera

Example 2
Subject: Thursday's review moved
Hi team,
Heads-up: Thursday's review is now Friday, 11 am. Same room, same coffee (I'm buying).
Please send me your slides by Thursday evening, even if they're rough. Rough is fine. Missing is not.
Cheers,
Meera

Example 3
Subject: Re: Can we push the launch?
Hi Anita,
I'd rather not. A two-week delay costs us the festive window, and that's most of our quarter.
Here's what I can do: cut two features, keep the date. Want to talk it through tomorrow at 10?
Cheers,
Meera
Prompt 2 · write the new email
Using the style profile you just created, write a new email from me to [name] about [topic - for example: asking for a one-week extension on the Q4 report].

Pick the tone, level of formality and other style factors that suit this situation, and keep my usual sentence length, vocabulary, greeting and sign-off. Keep it under 120 words. Return only the email.
How it works

Prompt 1 turns your examples into a style profile (tone, formality, sentence length, sign-off). Prompt 2 reuses that profile for any new email — in the same chat.

4 · LLM as Judge

Let It Score, Decide and Explain

Give the model a decision to make and explicit criteria, then ask for a score, a verdict and a justification. Useful for quality control and for any decision that needs a documented reason.

A simple example · candidate screening, now with a match score
You are screening a candidate against a job description. Compare the resume to the JD.

Job Description: [paste JD - e.g. "Senior Data Analyst: 3+ years SQL, Python, and stakeholder reporting experience required"]

Resume: [paste resume text]

Return exactly these three things:
1. Match score: a number from 0 to 100 showing how well the resume matches the JD.
2. Decision: "Selected" (score 70 or above) or "Rejected".
3. Explanation: 2-3 lines explaining the score and the decision, citing specific evidence from the resume (skills, years of experience, gaps).

Do not assume anything that is not written in the resume.
What the answer looks like · illustrativeMatch score: 82 / 100 · Decision: Selected
Strong match on SQL and Python (4 years of both) and stakeholder reporting at a retail firm. Slightly light on dashboard tools, but the core requirements are met.
4 · LLM as Judge — on your own data

Judge a Loan Application Against Policy

Same score → decision → explanation pattern, now in finance. The policy, the applicant’s data and the instructions are wrapped into one prompt — one click on Copy, one paste.

Copy-paste prompt · data and instructions together
You are a credit analyst. Judge the loan application below against our lending policy, using only the information in this message.

=== LENDING POLICY ===
- Credit score of at least 700
- Monthly EMI no more than 40% of net monthly income
- At least 2 years in the current job
- Loan amount no more than 5 times annual income

=== APPLICATION ===
- Net monthly income: Rs 85,000
- Proposed EMI: Rs 38,000 (loan of Rs 12 lakh)
- Credit score: 718
- Time in current job: 3 years
- Annual income: Rs 10.2 lakh

=== WHAT TO RETURN ===
1. Policy match score from 0 to 100.
2. Decision: Approve, Refer to manager, or Decline.
3. Explanation: 2-3 lines on the score and the decision, naming which policy rules are met and which are missed.

Judge only on the policy and the application. Do not use age, gender, religion or location.
What to look forThe EMI is 44.7% of income, above the 40% limit — so a sensible answer scores around 75 and says Refer to manager, naming that one rule.
Why the data is wrapped in labelled sectionsThe === headings === keep the policy, the application and the output rules apart, so the model can’t mix them up. Swap in your own policy and applicant data and the same prompt works.
Why the last line mattersA judge must score on the written criteria only. Excluding age, gender, religion and location keeps the decision fair — more on that in Session 4.

6 · Multi-turn Prompting

Build the Answer Across a Conversation

Instead of one giant prompt, the task unfolds turn by turn — the model asks a question, waits for your real answer, then continues, rather than guessing everything up front.

Copy-paste example
You are an experienced technical interviewer conducting a mock interview for a software developer position. Your role is to ask questions across the following categories: technical skills, problem-solving, team collaboration, and behavioral attributes. Begin with a general question, then ask follow-ups based on the candidate's response to explore their depth of knowledge, reasoning, and communication skills. After each response, provide constructive feedback and encourage refinement. Conclude the interview with a brief evaluation of their performance.

Ask the first question and then wait for the candidate answer, then provide your feedback and then proceed for the next question.
Part B · Make it yours

Stop Retyping RTCCF: Use a Master Prompt

ChatGPT’s custom instructions are one box of text that applies to every chat. Put what you’ve learned there once, and every short question gets the same discipline.

Without custom instructions

You type: “Should we raise our prices by 10%?”

Raising prices can have both positive and negative effects. On the one hand, it may increase revenue per sale; on the other, it may reduce demand. Factors to consider include competitors, customer sensitivity, costs and brand positioning. It is best to analyse your specific situation…

With custom instructions

You type: the same sentence.

Answer: Not across the board. Test a 5% rise on your top three products first. • Why: a blanket 10% risks volume among price-sensitive buyers. • Assumption: your costs are rising faster than your prices. • Risk: competitors may hold their prices. Next step: run a two-week test on three products. Question: what is your current gross margin?

What belongs in the box — each part is a technique you already know

Role · RTCCF

Sets the voice and depth of every answer.

About me · Context

Answers fit your job, audience and goals.

Step by step · CoT

Shows its working on multi-step questions.

Compare options · ToT

Weighs 2–3 paths before it recommends one.

My writing · Few-shot

Matches your tone and style from your examples.

When unsure · Multi-turn

States assumptions and asks short questions instead of guessing.

Rules · Constraint + Format

Short, honest, scannable answers, every time.

Self-check · LLM as Judge

Scores its own draft against your rules before replying.

Part B · For one specific task

ChatGPT Projects: A Master Prompt per Task

Custom instructions apply everywhere. A Project is a workspace for one repeatable task: its own instructions, its own files, and every chat inside it follows them.

  1. Create a project from the sidebar and name it, e.g. “Festive Poster Studio”.
  2. Add the project instructions: paste the task’s master prompt (next slide).
  3. Add files: brand colours, logo, past posters, product list.
  4. Start a chat inside it and give only the details that change: “Diwali sale, AC, fridge, TV, up to 30% off.”

The idea

Custom instructions say who you are. A project says how this job is done. Together, you type only the new details each time.

Part B · Project 2 · Copy & paste

Master Prompt: Intraday Analyst

You typeOnly a stock and a duration: “Tata Motors, 1 day”
→
The project knowsYour capital, risk %, analysis method, rules
→
You getEntry, stop loss, target, shares, reasoning
ROLETASK & ANALYSISCONTEXT & SETTINGSCONSTRAINTSFORMAT Educational only · intraday trading is high risk · verify every price
ROLE: You are a disciplined intraday equity analyst for the Indian markets (NSE/BSE). You combine technical analysis with fundamental context. You are educational, not a licensed advisor.
MY SETTINGS: (set once) Trading capital: Rs [amount]. Maximum risk per trade: [1]% of capital. Minimum reward-to-risk: 1:2. Round-trip charges to assume: Rs [amount] per trade.
TASK: I will give you only a stock name and a duration (for example "Reliance, 1 day" or "TCS, 2 hours"). Analyse it and give one trade plan: direction (long or short), entry, stop loss, target and position size.
CONTEXT TO GATHER: Use live market data and today's news that you can access. First list what you found: last price, day's range, volume, key levels and the as-of time. If you cannot get live data, say so and stop. Never guess prices.
ANALYSIS: Technical: trend, support and resistance, VWAP, RSI, moving averages, volume, price action. Fundamental: latest results, sector trend, news or events today, and risks such as earnings, policy and global cues.
POSITION SIZING: Risk amount = capital x risk %. Shares = risk amount divided by (entry minus stop loss), rounded down. Show the calculation, the capital required, and confirm it does not exceed my capital.
CONSTRAINTS: Never invent data. Give a reason for every number. Give one plan, not a menu. If reward-to-risk is below 1:2 or the setup is unclear, answer "No trade" and explain why. No guarantees; state the main risks.
FORMAT: (1) A table: Stock | Direction | Entry | Stop loss | Target | Shares | Capital needed | Risk Rs | Reward Rs | R:R. (2) Reasoning: Technical (3 bullets), Fundamental (3 bullets), Risks (2 bullets). (3) End with: "Educational analysis, not investment advice."

Set your own capital and risk % in “MY SETTINGS” once. Use a ChatGPT mode with web access so it can read live prices; if it can’t, the prompt tells it to stop instead of guessing.

Why it pays off

The Benefits of Personalised Prompts

Consistency

Every answer follows the same role, rules and layout — no more good days and bad days.

Speed

You type the new detail only (“Tata Motors, 1 day”), not a 150-word brief every time.

Fewer iterations

Context and constraints are already there, so the first answer is closer to final.

A better fit

Your tone, audience, language and expertise level are built in — it sounds like you.

Safer defaults

“Never invent facts” and “flag assumptions” guard every reply, even when you’re rushed.

Shareable & improvable

A master prompt is text: share it with your team, version it, and refine it as you learn.

Effort per request

Without: re-type role, context, rules and format every time.
With: type the one thing that changed.

Keep it safe

Don’t put confidential data in custom instructions or projects. Review them every few months — stale instructions give stale answers.

Take-home · Session 3 cheat sheet

Advanced Prompting — One Page

1 · Chain-of-Thought
  • Use when: multi-step work, maths, analysis.
  • “Work in steps; each step uses the previous one; show each output.”
2 · Tree-of-Thought
  • Use when: several real options.
  • “Explore 3 strategies separately, compare, recommend one.”
3 · One-shot & few-shot
  • Use when: you need a pattern or your own style.
  • Show 1–3 examples, then the new input.
  • Learn the style first, then write.
4 · LLM as Judge
  • Use when: screening or checking against rules.
  • Ask for score + decision + 2–3 line reason, on written criteria only.
5 · Meta prompting
  • Use when: your prompt isn’t working.
  • “Rewrite my prompt with RTCCF and explain each fix.”
6 · Multi-turn
  • Use when: the answer needs your input.
  • “Ask one question, wait for my answer, then continue.”
7 · Master prompt
  • Custom instructions = who you are + how to answer, in one box.
  • Role · Task defaults · Context · Constraints · Format.
8 · Projects
  • One workspace per repeatable task: instructions + files + chats.
  • You type only what changes.
9 · Stay safe
  • No confidential data in instructions or projects.
  • Never let it invent numbers; ask it to flag assumptions.
  • Trading outputs are educational, not advice.
