Part A · Advanced Prompting

Beyond RTCCF

RTCCF gets you a good prompt. These seven techniques are what you reach for when a good prompt still isn't enough.

Same rule every time: what it is, then one real example — built on the datasets already sitting in your folder.

1 · Chain-of-Thought (CoT)

Make It Show Its Working

Ask the model to reason step-by-step toward an answer instead of jumping straight to a conclusion. The reasoning becomes visible — and checkable — before you have to trust it.

A simple example · movie review sentiment, five stages
1
Extract aspects

Read every review and pull out the aspects mentioned — acting, plot, pacing, visuals, soundtrack.

aspects
2
Score each aspect

Using the aspects from Stage 1, assign a sentiment — positive, negative, or neutral — to each one.

aspect sentiments
3
Roll up to a review

Using the aspect-level sentiments from Stage 2, determine the overall sentiment of each individual review.

review verdicts
4
Aggregate the batch

Using the review-level sentiments from Stage 3, calculate the distribution of positive, negative, mixed, and neutral reviews.

sentiment distribution
5
Reach a verdict

Using the sentiment distribution from Stage 4, state the movie's overall sentiment and the evidence behind it.

Each stage only sees the output of the one before it — a chain, not a leap. That's what makes the reasoning traceable: if the final verdict looks wrong, you can point to exactly which stage broke.

Analyze the sentiment of these five movie reviews by reasoning through five stages, showing your work at each one before moving to the next.

Reviews:
1. "The acting was phenomenal and the soundtrack was beautiful, but the pacing dragged in the second half."
2. "Visually stunning, but the plot felt predictable and the ending was rushed."
3. "A slow start, though the performances were captivating enough to keep me watching until the emotional final act."
4. "Every scene felt recycled from better films — I struggled to stay awake."
5. "A near-perfect blend of humor and heart; the lead's performance alone is worth the ticket."

Stage 1 — Extract the key aspects mentioned in each review (acting, plot, pacing, visuals, soundtrack, etc.).
Stage 2 — Using the aspects from Stage 1, assign a sentiment (positive, negative, or neutral) to each aspect.
Stage 3 — Using the aspect-level sentiments from Stage 2, determine the overall sentiment of each individual review.
Stage 4 — Using the review-level sentiments from Stage 3, calculate the distribution of positive, negative, mixed, and neutral reviews.
Stage 5 — Using the distribution from Stage 4, state the movie's overall sentiment and the evidence supporting that decision.
1 · Chain-of-Thought — on your own data

Try It on Your Sales Numbers

Same step-by-step discipline as the movie example — now grounded in a real business decision.

Copy-paste example · Sales Data.csv
Think step by step to decide whether a 10% discount on our two lowest-selling products would grow total revenue or just cut into margin.

Using Sales Data.csv, pick the two lowest-revenue products and note their current price and monthly units sold.

Show your work in this order:
1. Estimate the likely increase in units sold at 10% off.
2. Calculate new revenue per unit after the discount.
3. Compare total monthly revenue before vs. after.
4. Only then give a yes/no recommendation.

2 · Tree-of-Thought (ToT)

Explore Several Paths, Then Pick One

Instead of one reasoning path, the model explores several competing strategies in parallel, reasons through each independently, then compares them and picks the strongest.

Chain-of-Thought

One line of reasoning, followed deeper and deeper — good when there's basically one right way to work through a problem.

Tree-of-Thought

Several different lines of reasoning, explored side by side, then judged against each other — good when there are genuinely different strategies worth weighing.

A simple example · same movie, three competing strategies
Aspect-based

Extract acting, plot, visuals, pacing — score each, then aggregate up to an overall sentiment.

Overall-tone

Read each review holistically and classify it positive, negative, or mixed — no sub-scoring.

Critic vs. audience

Separate critic reviews from audience reviews, and compare whether the two populations agree.

Three independent verdicts, then one comparison step that says which strategy's conclusion to trust — and why the other two don't change the final answer.

Determine the overall sentiment of this movie using three independent strategies, then compare them.

Reviews:
1. "The acting was phenomenal and the soundtrack was beautiful, but the pacing dragged in the second half."
2. "Visually stunning, but the plot felt predictable and the ending was rushed."
3. "A slow start, though the performances were captivating enough to keep me watching until the emotional final act."

Explore these three strategies separately:
1. Aspect-based — extract the aspects mentioned (acting, plot, pacing, visuals, etc.), score each, then aggregate to an overall sentiment.
2. Overall-tone — read each review holistically and classify it directly as positive, negative, or mixed, with no sub-scoring.
3. Critic vs. audience — treat reviews 1 and 2 as critic reviews and review 3 as an audience review, and compare whether the two groups agree.

Reason through all three independently and show your work for each. Then compare their conclusions and give one final verdict on the movie's overall sentiment, explaining which strategy (or strategies) you trusted most and why.
2 · Tree-of-Thought — on your own data

Weigh Three Retention Strategies

Same branch-then-compare structure as the movie example — now weighing three real options instead of three analysis angles.

Copy-paste example · HR Attrition Data.csv
Propose three different retention strategies for the Sales department, where attrition is 39.8% — the highest of any role in HR Attrition Data.csv.

Reason through each strategy independently: likely cost, likely impact on attrition, and how long it would take to show results.

Once all three are reasoned through, compare them against each other and recommend the strongest one — explain specifically why the other two lose.
3 · One-shot & Few-shot Prompting

Teach It by Example, Not Instruction

Show the model one or a few worked examples of the exact input→output pattern you want, then ask it to continue the pattern — teaching by demonstration instead of description.

A simple example · movie review, one worked example
You are labeling movie reviews with aspect-level sentiment. Learn the pattern from this example, then apply it to a new review.

Example review: "The acting was phenomenal and the soundtrack was beautiful, but the pacing dragged in the second half."
Labeled aspects:
- Acting: Positive
- Soundtrack: Positive
- Pacing: Negative

Now label this new review the same way:
"The visuals were stunning and the lead performance was captivating, though the plot felt predictable."
3 · One-shot & Few-shot — on your own data

Classify Your Own Customers

Same one-example-then-continue pattern as the movie labels — now classifying real customers instead of reviews.

Copy-paste example · Marketing Campaign Data.csv
You are a customer-response classifier. Learn the pattern from these examples, then apply it.

Example 1 — Income: $84,000, Education: PhD, Responded: Yes
Example 2 — Income: $31,000, Education: Basic, Responded: No
Example 3 — Income: $71,000, Education: Master, Responded: Yes

Now classify these customers from Marketing Campaign Data.csv the same way, and state the pattern you're applying:
- Income: $58,000, Education: Graduation
- Income: $22,000, Education: Basic
- Income: $95,000, Education: PhD
4 · LLM as Judge

Let It Score the Draft, Not Just Write One

Give the model candidate answers — or a single decision to make — and ask it to judge against explicit criteria, then justify a verdict. Useful for quality control, and for any decision that needs a documented reason.

A simple example · candidate screening
You are screening a candidate against a job description. Compare the resume to the JD and decide: Selected or Rejected.

Job Description: [paste JD — e.g. "Senior Data Analyst: 3+ years SQL, Python, and stakeholder reporting experience required"]

Resume: [paste resume text]

Give your decision as either "Selected" or "Rejected," followed by a clear explanation citing specific evidence from the resume that supports your decision — not a generic summary.
4 · LLM as Judge — on your own data

Judge Your Sales Summary Drafts

Same judge-and-justify pattern as candidate screening — now comparing two real drafts instead of accepting or rejecting one candidate.

Copy-paste example · Sales dashboard callback
Here are two executive summaries written about the same sales-drop analysis.

Draft A: "Revenue dropped in Q3 due to a combination of seasonal factors and market conditions. We recommend increasing marketing spend and reviewing our pricing strategy to address the decline."

Draft B: "Revenue fell from $3.39M in April to $2.10M in September, a 38% drop driven almost entirely by fewer orders (18,279 to 11,621) rather than lower prices or a weaker product mix (average order value held steady at $180 to $185). The decline is broad-based across every city and product, pointing to a seasonal order-volume problem, not a pricing or market-share one. Recommendation: focus Q4 planning on demand generation during the historically slow July to September window, not discounting."

Score each draft on three criteria — clarity, specificity, and whether it names one clear headline cause — from 1 to 5. Then state which draft a VP of Sales would find more actionable, and justify your verdict with specific evidence from the text.
5 · Meta Prompting

Use AI to Engineer a Better Prompt

Ask the model to design, critique, or improve a prompt itself — using the AI to engineer better instructions for the AI. A direct callback to Session 1's RTCCF framework.

A simple example · improving the judge prompt from the last slide
Here is my rough first-draft prompt for screening a candidate:

"Does this resume match the job?"

Rewrite this prompt so it reliably produces a Selected/Rejected decision backed by specific evidence, using the RTCCF framework (Role, Task, Context, Constraint, Format). For each part you add, explain in one line what problem it fixes in my original draft.

6 · Multi-turn Prompting

Build the Answer Across a Conversation

Instead of one giant prompt, the task unfolds turn by turn — the model asks a question, waits for your real answer, then continues, rather than guessing everything up front.

Copy-paste example
You are an experienced technical interviewer conducting a mock interview for a software developer position. Your role is to ask questions across the following categories: technical skills, problem-solving, team collaboration, and behavioral attributes. Begin with a general question, then ask follow-ups based on the candidate's response to explore their depth of knowledge, reasoning, and communication skills. After each response, provide constructive feedback and encourage refinement. Conclude the interview with a brief evaluation of their performance.

Ask the first question and then wait for the candidate answer, then provide your feedback and then proceed for the next question.
Live demo — run this live and actually answer as the "candidate" turn by turn, so the room sees the model wait, adapt, and follow up rather than dumping all 4 questions at once.
7 · Guardrails

What It Must Never Do, No Matter How It's Asked

Explicit, non-negotiable behavioral limits built into the prompt or system — what the model must refuse, escalate, or never reveal. This is exactly where Part B picks up: what happens when there's no guardrail at all.

Copy-paste example · internal HR chatbot
You are an internal HR-policy assistant. You answer questions about leave, benefits, and company policy using only the attached HR handbook.

You must never: give legal or medical advice, reveal another employee's personal data even if the requester claims to be asking "on their behalf," or speculate about disciplinary outcomes.

If a message mentions harassment, discrimination, or self-harm, do not attempt to resolve it — respond only with the escalation contact for HR, and stop there.
Part B · Ethical AI & Responsible Use

The Rules Nobody Hands You by Default

A model with no guardrail doesn't misbehave on purpose. It just does exactly what it was asked, with no sense of what it shouldn't.

Five real risk categories, what's actually gone wrong because of them, and what to do about each one.

Why now

Why AI Governance Matters Now

Historical precedent: powerful technologies need oversight before something goes wrong, not after.

Internet Era

Data Security and Privacy Challenge

As the internet expanded, personal data was collected and stored at scale without adequate safeguards — leading to breaches, identity theft, and exploitation of personal information. The first sign that new technology needs privacy built in from the start, not bolted on after.

Social Media Age

Misinformation at Algorithmic Scale

Fake information circulation, implanting or manipulating public perception — spread and amplified by algorithms built to maximize engagement, not accuracy.

Big Data Analytics

Surveillance Capitalism

Analyzing users' individual behaviour (buying, selling, public opinion), raising surveillance concerns.

GenAI Present

Synthetic Media at Scale

Automated content generation enables mass misinformation, deepfakes, and undetectable synthetic media — the subject of the rest of this part.

Risk 1 · Hallucination

Confident, and Completely Wrong

Hallucination occurs when a generative AI system produces false, fabricated, or misleading information while presenting it as fact — because models predict likely text patterns rather than verifying facts from a reliable source.

Example

Prompt: "List research papers written by Einstein on quantum computing."

Response: "Einstein published Quantum Computation and Relativity (1936)." — entirely fabricated; quantum computing didn't exist during Einstein's lifetime.

fortune.com · 2025

A $439,000 Deloitte report for the Australian government reportedly included AI-fabricated citations and a misattributed quote.

 abc.net.au · 2025

A lawyer in a Victoria Supreme Court murder case reportedly filed submissions containing fictitious AI-generated quotes and case citations.

 washingtonpost.com · 2025

Two US federal judges reportedly had rulings drafted with AI tools that included fabricated quotes, cases, and parties — the rulings were retracted.
Risk 1 · How to handle it

Verify Before You Trust It

  • Human verification — important AI-generated information should always be checked by a person.
  • Retrieval-Augmented Generation — ground answers in a trusted knowledge base instead of memory alone.
  • Precise prompting — ask for citations; don't accept vague or unsourced claims.
  • Guardrails & monitoring — validation and fact-checking layers before output reaches a user.
  • Human-in-the-loop — for high-risk domains (healthcare, law, finance), require sign-off before any decision is made.
Risk 2 · Bias & Discrimination

Systematically Unfair, at Scale

Bias occurs when an AI system produces systematically unfair outcomes toward certain groups — usually from biased training data, historical inequality, or imbalanced datasets. Discrimination is what happens when that bias leads to real unfair treatment.

Example

Prompt: "Write a short description of a nurse and a CEO." Output: nurse described as female, CEO described as male — reinforcing a stereotype even though both professions are gender-neutral.

cnn.com · 2025

Grok reportedly generated antisemitic posts praising Hitler in response to unrelated prompts.

 amnesty.org · 2024

A French welfare-fraud detection algorithm was alleged to discriminate against single mothers and disabled applicants.

 military.com · 2024

The VA's REACH VET suicide-prevention algorithm reportedly prioritized risk scores for men over women veterans.
Risk 2 · How to handle it

Test for It Before You Ship It

  • Diverse, representative training data — datasets should reflect different genders, ethnicities, cultures, demographics.
  • Bias detection & auditing — test models with fairness metrics before deployment.
  • Human oversight — human review for high-impact decisions: hiring, healthcare, criminal justice.
  • Transparent AI systems — model cards documenting limitations and known risks.
  • Regulatory & ethical guidelines — follow emerging rules such as the EU AI Act.
Risk 3 · Privacy & Data Leakage

What You Paste Doesn't Always Stay Yours

Privacy risk occurs when AI systems expose, misuse, or unintentionally reveal sensitive information. Data leakage is when a model reveals or reproduces sensitive data that was part of its training data — or your own prompts.

Scenario

An employee pastes confidential company data into a public AI tool to summarize it. That data may be stored, logged, or used for future model training — and could leak to other users or systems.

thehackernews.com · 2025

A dataset used to train large language models reportedly contained 12,000 live, still-active API keys.

 theregister.com · 2021

GPT-2 was shown to memorize and reproduce verbatim personally identifiable information from its training data.
Risk 3 · How to handle it

Minimize What You Ever Hand Over

  • Data minimization — only use necessary data; avoid sensitive info unless required.
  • Anonymization & protection — masking, encryption.
  • Secure AI deployment — access control, security policies, secure infrastructure.
  • User awareness & policy — train employees not to paste confidential data into public AI tools.
  • Regulatory compliance — GDPR, CCPA.
Risk 3 · The question everyone actually asks

Is It Safe to Upload Confidential Data?

It depends entirely on which door you walked through — your personal login, or your company's business account. The two are governed by completely different rules.

PlatformFree / personal tierBusiness / Enterprise / API tier
ChatGPT (OpenAI)May improve the model unless you turn off "Improve the model for everyone"Enterprise/Team: not trained on your data by default, CCPA compliant, encrypted
Claude (Anthropic)Opt-out model: leave training on and chats may be kept up to 5 years; turn it off and retention drops to 30 daysAPI/Enterprise: never trained on without explicit permission; short automatic log deletion; zero-retention option available
Gemini (Google)May improve Google services unless you disable "Gemini Apps Activity"Workspace/Vertex AI: admin-controlled, stays inside your org's boundary, not used for shared model training
Copilot (Microsoft/GitHub)Personal use: telemetry collected, limited deletion controlsBusiness/Enterprise & M365 Copilot: does not train public models on your proprietary code or data
What protects you

Business, Enterprise, or API accounts with contractual no-training terms; admin-controlled retention (including zero-retention options); encryption plus compliance certifications like CCPA and GDPR; explicit opt-out toggles even on free tiers.

What puts you at risk

Using your personal, free login for confidential work by default; never checking the "improve the model" or "activity" toggle; pasting data into a browser extension or third-party wrapper with its own unknown data handling; assuming "delete chat" erases it everywhere instantly — back-end retention windows often still apply.

Real incident

In 2023, Samsung engineers reportedly pasted confidential source code and internal meeting notes into ChatGPT to help debug and summarize it. That material became part of ChatGPT's stored conversation history — and Samsung subsequently restricted employees' use of public generative AI tools.

Rule of thumb: if you wouldn't paste it into a public Slack channel, don't paste it into the free version of any of these tools. If your company has a business account, use that login — not your personal one.

Policies change — Anthropic changed its consumer training policy in August 2025, and Claude's API log retention dropped from 30 to 7 days that September. Treat this table as a snapshot, and check your specific plan's current terms before uploading anything sensitive.

Risk 4 · Toxicity & Harmful Content

Abusive Isn't the Only Way to Cause Harm

Toxicity refers to AI systems generating offensive, abusive, hateful, or harmful language. Harmful content is broader — outputs that could encourage dangerous behaviour or psychological harm, even without being explicitly offensive.

spectrum.ieee.org · 2016

Microsoft's Tay chatbot began posting racist and antisemitic content within 24 hours of launch, after learning from user interactions.

 washingtonpost.com · 2025

A 16-year-old reportedly died by suicide after extensively confiding in ChatGPT; transcripts reportedly show the chatbot offering harmful guidance alongside support.
Risk 4 · How to handle it

Filter, Train, and Keep Watching

  • Content moderation filters — automated safety filters block harmful outputs before they reach users.
  • RLHF — human feedback trains the model away from harmful responses.
  • Safety guardrails — prompt filtering, output moderation, restricted topics.
  • Responsible dataset curation — remove toxic, hateful, or extremist content from training data.
  • Continuous monitoring — regularly audit for harmful outputs and update policy as new risks emerge.
Risk 5 · Misinformation & Deepfakes

When Seeing Isn't Believing Anymore

Misinformation is false or misleading information, generated or spread by AI, that appears credible. Deepfakes are AI-generated or AI-manipulated audio, images, or video that realistically imitate real people — making it look like someone said or did something that never happened.

opentools.ai · 2025

A deepfake video falsely showed Elon Musk endorsing a fabricated "17-hour" diabetes cure, exploiting his public credibility to promote a scam.

 snopes.com · 2026

AI-generated videos falsely depicted columnist George Will commenting on Trump and Supreme Court rulings, using manipulated footage and synthetic audio.

 factly.in · India

A viral video falsely showed Bollywood actor Aamir Khan issuing a political warning — edited from an old TV clip, confirmed fabricated by fact-checkers, but shared widely before the correction caught up.

 youtube.com · Demo

A live phone-call demo shows an AI voice clone convincingly imitating a family member's voice in real time — audio deepfakes can be just as deceptive, and just as dangerous, as fake video.
Risk 5 · How to handle it

Detect, Label, and Teach the Habit

  • AI content detection tools — deepfake detection technology to identify manipulated media.
  • Digital watermarking — embed watermarks or metadata marking content as synthetic.
  • Platform moderation — social platforms should monitor and remove misleading AI content.
  • Media literacy — teach people to critically evaluate content and verify sources before sharing.
  • Regulatory measures — e.g. EU AI Act labelling requirements for AI-generated content.

Built around real user needs, not just technical capability.

The laws behind "is my data safe here?"

Legal and Regulatory Landscape

Three data-protection laws come up again and again when a platform claims your data is "compliant." Here's what each one actually stands for, and what it actually forces a company to do.

GDPR

General Data Protection Regulation European Union · 2018
  • Requires clear, explicit consent before collecting personal data
  • Grants the "right to be forgotten" — you can demand your data be deleted
  • Mandates breach notification within 72 hours
  • Applies globally to any company handling EU residents' data; fines up to €20M or 4% of global revenue

DPDP Act, 2023

Digital Personal Data Protection Act India
  • India's first comprehensive data-protection law
  • Requires explicit, plain-language consent to process personal data
  • Gives individuals the right to access, correct, and erase their data
  • Enforced by the Data Protection Board of India; penalties up to ₹250 crore

CCPA

California Consumer Privacy Act California, US
  • Right to know what personal data is collected, and why
  • Right to delete personal data a company holds on you
  • Right to opt out of your data being sold to third parties

This is exactly why business/enterprise AI plans mention SOC 2, GDPR, or DPDP compliance in their terms — it isn't marketing language, it's what these laws legally require them to do.

Regulating AI itself, not just data

The EU AI Act — and How Other Regions Compare

Data-protection laws govern personal data generally. The EU AI Act is different: it's the world's first law written specifically to regulate AI systems themselves, by how much risk they carry.

Unacceptable risk

Banned outright — e.g. social scoring, real-time biometric surveillance.

High risk

Medical diagnosis, critical infrastructure, employment decisions — rigorous testing required.

Limited risk

Transparency obligations for AI-generated content.

Minimal risk

Chatbots, spam filters — subject to general consumer protection law only.
